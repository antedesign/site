The Double Diamond is two decisions, not four phases.

Four phases are the part of the model that is easy to put on a slide. The two points where the diamonds narrow are where the budget is committed, and they are usually the least documented moments in the whole project.

Method · 14 September 2026 · 10 min read · By Anté
Method · 01

Almost every team that draws the Double Diamond draws four phases. That is the part of the model that reproduces cleanly on a slide, fits in a project plan and survives being explained in thirty seconds. It is also the part that does the least work.

The content of the model is in two places on the drawing, and neither of them is a phase. They are the two points where the shape narrows: the moment a team commits to a statement of the problem, and the moment it commits to one direction and drops the others. Everything else on the diagram is preparation for those two moments, or execution of what they decided.

Where the model comes from, and one date almost everyone gets wrong

Most write-ups will tell you the Double Diamond was published in 2005, based on a study of design at eleven global companies. The Design Council's own history of the model says something different.

The model was created in 2003 by the Council's in-house Design and Innovation team, led by Richard Eisermann, and first shared publicly in 2004. It came out of reviewing projects the Council had run itself, not out of a field study of other people's companies.

The eleven companies belong to a different document. Eleven lessons: managing design in eleven global brands was published in 2007 and looked at how design was run inside firms including LEGO, Microsoft, Sony, Starbucks and Virgin Atlantic. The Double Diamond appeared in it as a framing device. Over twenty years of retelling, the two got fused into one origin story.

This matters for more than accuracy. It tells you what kind of object the model is. The Double Diamond was never a research finding about how innovation succeeds. It was a description of a shape that kept showing up, drawn so it could be communicated. The underlying idea is older again: alternating divergence and convergence comes from Béla H. Bánáthy's Designing Social Systems in a Changing World, published in 1996. What the Double Diamond added was the second diamond, and with it the insistence that you do this twice, once on the problem and once on the solution.

The Council has since moved on twice. The Framework for Innovation (2019) made the process explicitly non-linear and wrapped principles and leadership around it. The Systemic Design Framework (2021) reframed the work as Explore, Reframe, Create and Catalyse. The neat four-phase diamond most teams still work from is the 2004 drawing.

What the model actually says

Two diamonds, side by side. The first covers the problem: Discover opens up to gather evidence about what is going on, Define narrows that down to a statement of the problem worth solving. The second covers the solution: Develop opens up again to generate ways of solving it, Deliver narrows to the one you commit to.

Each diamond has the same rhythm. Widen, then narrow. Explore, then choose.

The part everyone draws, and the part that does the work

Look at the drawing again and ignore the words for a moment. What is left is two wide openings and two narrow points.

The openings are activity: interviews, desk research, workshops, sketches, prototypes. Activity is visible. It can be scheduled, staffed, reported on and shown to a steering committee. It generates artefacts, and artefacts feel like progress.

The narrow points are something else entirely. At the end of the first diamond, a group of people commits to a sentence about what the problem is. At the end of the second, it commits to one direction and lets the alternatives go. Those two commitments determine where every euro after them is spent. The first decides what the project is about. The second decides what gets built.

Divergence is visible. Convergence is where the money is committed, and it is usually the least documented moment in the project.

This is why "we ran a Double Diamond" and "we made two defensible decisions" are not the same claim, and why the first is considerably easier to make.

Four ways it breaks in practice

1. It gets read as a timeline

The diamonds are drawn left to right, so they get transcribed into a schedule: two weeks Discover, one week Define, three weeks Develop. Phases can be planned, which is exactly what makes this attractive. The model then behaves like a waterfall with better geometry.

The 2019 Framework for Innovation states plainly that the process is not linear. In practice, a team that learns something during Develop that undermines its problem statement has two options: reopen the first diamond, or carry on. Once the phases are in a plan with dates attached, that becomes a political question rather than an evidential one.

Signal to watch for: the problem statement has not changed since week two, and nobody can recall it being discussed since.

2. "Diverge" gets read as brainstorming

In the first diamond, divergence means going out and finding out. Interviews, observation, data you did not previously have. It is evidence gathering, and it is slow.

What often happens instead is a room, a wall and a pack of sticky notes. That produces divergence in the opinions the team already held. You finish with more options and no more information.

The test is blunt: after a divergent session, can you name one thing you now know that you did not know before, and say where it came from? If the answer is a list of ideas rather than a list of findings, you diverged inside the room, not outside it.

3. The first diamond gets skipped, because the brief already contains the answer

Most projects arrive with a solution already named: an app, a portal, a platform, a rebrand. When the solution is in the brief, Discover and Define quietly change function. They become research about the solution rather than research about the problem. The shape still gets drawn on the kick-off slide. The first diamond is decorative.

This is the most expensive of the four, because it is invisible. You cannot see the problem you never framed, and the project will not fail in a way that points back at it. It will fail later, as weak adoption, as scope that keeps expanding, as a rebuild eighteen months on.

4. Convergence happens without criteria

The most common version: options first, criteria afterwards. A team develops three concepts, puts them side by side, and then builds the comparison that explains the one it already prefers. The criteria get written to fit the conclusion. Because they are written in good faith, nobody notices.

The order matters more than the content. Criteria set before the options exist are a decision rule. The same criteria written after the options exist are a justification. They can be word for word identical and still do opposite jobs.

What the model does not give you

Three things are missing from the drawing, and all three have to be supplied by whoever is running the work.

  • A stopping rule. Nothing in the model says when Discover ends. In the absence of a rule, "enough" gets decided by the calendar or by fatigue, whichever arrives first.
  • An evidence standard. The model treats one senior stakeholder's opinion, a competitor scan and six user interviews as the same kind of input. They are not. Nothing in the shape distinguishes what you know from what you assume.
  • Decision rights. The diamonds do not say who converges. In most organisations the default answer is whoever is most senior in the room at the time, which is not the same as whoever has the best evidence.

These are not flaws. The Double Diamond was drawn to describe a shape, not to govern a decision. They become flaws the moment the drawing is used as a process.

How to run it so it holds

Seven changes, none of which require a bigger budget. They move the weight of the work from the phases onto the two decisions.

  1. Write the assumed solution down first, then set it aside. Name the thing everyone is already picturing, in one sentence, before Discover starts. Making it explicit is what allows you to test it instead of serving it.
  2. Give divergence an evidence target, not a time box. "Six stakeholder interviews across three functions" is checkable. "Two weeks of discovery" is not.
  3. Separate what you know from what you assume. Two columns, maintained as you go. Anything in the assumption column that would change the decision if it turned out to be wrong is a research priority, in that order.
  4. Set the convergence criteria before you generate options. Write down what would make a direction the right one, and get the people who will sign off to agree with it, while there is still nothing to defend.
  5. Make the problem statement falsifiable. If no piece of evidence could show your problem statement to be wrong, you have written a topic, not a problem.
  6. Develop directions that differ in principle, not in degree. Three variations of one idea is one option shown three times. Directions built on genuinely different premises force a real trade-off, and the trade-off is what makes the choice defensible six months later.
  7. Record why the others were dropped. A decision nobody can reconstruct will be reopened. Usually at the worst possible moment.
The short version

Spend less effort proving you followed the process and more effort making the two convergence points explicit: what the problem is, and what would make one direction better than another. Both should be written down before the evidence that supports them is collected.

Common questions

What are the four stages of the Double Diamond?

Discover, Define, Develop and Deliver. Discover and Define sit in the first diamond and deal with the problem. Develop and Deliver sit in the second and deal with the solution. Each diamond widens to explore and narrows to a decision.

Who created the Double Diamond, and in what year?

The UK Design Council's in-house Design and Innovation team created it in 2003, led by Richard Eisermann, and first shared it publicly in 2004.

It is frequently dated to 2005 and attributed to a study of eleven global companies. That conflates it with a separate Design Council report, Eleven lessons: managing design in eleven global brands, published in 2007.

What is the difference between the Double Diamond and design thinking?

Design thinking is a broad family of human-centred practices. The Double Diamond is one diagram of the shape those practices tend to take: diverge then converge, once on the problem and once on the solution. It is more specific about sequence and less specific about method.

Is the Double Diamond still current?

The Design Council has published two successors: the Framework for Innovation in 2019, which makes the process explicitly non-linear, and the Systemic Design Framework in 2021, which reframes the work as Explore, Reframe, Create and Catalyse.

The original four-phase diamond remains the version most teams use, which is part of why the misreadings above are so persistent.

What is the most common mistake teams make with it?

Converging without criteria. Options get generated first and the comparison gets written afterwards, so the criteria describe the preferred option rather than test it. Criteria written before the options exist are a decision rule. The same criteria written afterwards are a justification.

Apply it to your own project

Use AI as a sparring partner, not an oracle

This prompt does not decide anything for you. It puts pressure on the two decisions above: whether your problem statement is a problem or a symptom, and whether your criteria were written before or after your preferred answer. Paste it into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini, and fill in the bracketed parts with your own project.

You are acting as a critical design and innovation sparring partner. Do not make the decision for me and do not hand me a recommendation. Your job is to find where my thinking is thin.

MY PROJECT
[one or two sentences: what you are working on, and who it is for]

THE PROBLEM AS I CURRENTLY STATE IT
[one sentence]

WHAT I KNOW (name the source for every line)
[bullet points: interviews, observation, data]

WHAT I AM ASSUMING (no source yet)
[bullet points]

THE DIRECTIONS I AM CONSIDERING
[list them]

THE DIRECTION I AM LEANING TOWARDS, AND WHY
[one paragraph]

Work through the following in order, answering each one separately.

1. PROBLEM OR SYMPTOM
Is my problem statement a root problem, or a symptom of one? Ask "why is that" up to five times in sequence, writing the most likely answer at each layer based on what I gave you. Tell me at which layer the problem becomes worth solving, and why the layers above it are not.

2. FALSIFIABILITY
What evidence would prove my problem statement wrong? If you cannot name any, say so plainly and rewrite the statement so that it can be wrong.

3. EVIDENCE AUDIT
Go through my "what I know" list and label each line: observed (someone saw or measured it), reported (someone said it), or inferred (we concluded it). Flag anything I have filed as knowledge that is actually inference.

4. THE SOLUTION ALREADY IN THE BRIEF
From how I described the project, name the solution I appear to have decided on before framing the problem. Quote the words that gave it away.

5. RETROFITTED CRITERIA
Look at the direction I am leaning towards. Write out the criteria I would have needed in order to arrive there. Then tell me whether those criteria look chosen for the problem, or chosen for the answer.

6. VARIATION OR ALTERNATIVE
Are my directions genuinely different in principle, or three versions of one idea? If they are versions, propose two directions that solve the same problem on a fundamentally different premise.

7. THE FIVE QUESTIONS
End with the five questions I must answer with real evidence, from people outside my team, before any budget is committed. Rank them by how much the decision changes if the answer surprises me.

Be specific and direct. Where I have given you too little to judge something, say what is missing instead of filling it in yourself.

Two notes on using it. Answer the bracketed sections properly before you paste: a model given thin input returns confident, thin output, and confidence is the part that travels. And when it pushes back on something you are sure about, that is the part worth reading twice.
